# I asked for a writing skill. Then I asked it to write.

I wanted a reusable writing skill. Something I could give a rough draft to and get back a piece that sounded closer to how I wanted to write.

The first version had a detailed voice profile, examples, a list of phrases to avoid, and a checklist. It had also passed the file checks. The instructions were readable, the references worked, and the skill was installed.

But had anyone actually used it to write something?

I asked for a subagent to try it. That gave us actual drafts to inspect instead of another explanation of why the instructions should work.

One of the tests asked for a short personal post. There were only a few supplied facts and a minimum word count. The draft stayed within the requested length and didn't invent a new event. It repeated the same observations in the second paragraph.

That was a useful failure. A word-count check could accept the piece while a reader would notice that it wasn't going anywhere. The next paragraph needed to add something, and there wasn't another supported detail to add.

The revised instructions dealt with that directly. Keep the facts. Remove the recap. If the notes can't support the requested length, say so rather than filling the space. A separate test with fuller notes produced a complete post without needing that repetition.

The research drafts had a different problem. They preserved the measurements and their limits, but kept arriving at a similar conclusion: this deserves more investigation. That can be a reasonable judgment. It also becomes a convenient ending when the writing hasn't explained what the result actually means.

The profile needed an example of making the local judgment clearly. Explain the mechanism, say what the evidence supports, and put the limitation beside that claim. A reader should be able to understand the result before being handed a list of things the study didn't test.

We also checked whether rewrites kept the original first-person voice, whether an exact quotation survived unchanged, and whether spelling preferences left configuration identifiers intact. Those details matter when the task is to rewrite someone's text. Cleaning up a paragraph shouldn't quietly change who did the work or alter a literal value.

I also wanted the skill to stand on its own. The writing patterns were useful. Keeping a person's name attached to them wasn't necessary for what I wanted to do. We removed the identifying references and kept the guidance general.

The final test batches found no new issues. That's enough to move to my own reading, not enough to assume every future draft will sound right.

Now I have a complete piece to read. I can check where the explanation feels natural, where it repeats itself, and which sentences I would actually keep. That's the next test I wanted.
